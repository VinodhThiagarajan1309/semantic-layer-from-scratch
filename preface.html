Preface

What we are building

The finished system, the route to it, and how to read this book.

At the end of this book you will type a sentence into a small program on your laptop: "Who are our top spenders in Wisconsin?" A few seconds later it answers: Carolyn Inworth, of Milwaukee, who has spent $502,529.42. That answer won't come from a report, an export, or an ETL job. It is computed live from two databases that have never heard of each other: her name and address from Aurora PostgreSQL, her purchases from Amazon Redshift. This book is about the layer in the middle that makes that possible, and you are going to build it yourself, one piece at a time.

You already know databases well: foreign keys, why a JOIN across a network hurts, what an ETL pipeline costs to keep alive, how many definitions of "active customer" one company can accumulate. What you probably haven't met is the other tradition of data modeling: RDF, ontologies, SPARQL, reasoning. Its academic reputation is undeserved. Underneath the vocabulary are a handful of practical ideas, each mapping onto something you already do in SQL, sometimes neatly and sometimes with an important twist. We'll learn them in the order you need them to build a working system.

P.1The end state, in one picture

Here is the finished system. Press Play to follow one question through it. Every box is something you'll build or inspect, labeled with its chapter.

Read it from the top. The agent, a short Python program, hands your English question to a large language model (an OpenAI model, or any model reachable through OpenRouter, such as Claude). The model doesn't know your data, but it's given a compact description of your business vocabulary (what a Customer is, what an Order is, how they connect) and one tool, run_sparql(query). It writes a SPARQL query and calls the tool.

The query arrives at Stardog, the semantic layer. Stardog holds no copies of your customers or orders. It holds meaning: business definitions, a few rules (what counts as a "big spender"), security policies, and mappings that say how each concept corresponds to real tables. Stardog works out which parts of the query concern Aurora and which Redshift, rewrites each as SQL, runs both, joins the results itself, and sends back rows. The model turns rows into a sentence.

Think of Stardog as a smart, shared view layer over several databases at once. Like a SQL view, it stores definitions, not data. Unlike a SQL view, its definitions use business vocabulary (Customer, Order, Big Spender) rather than column names, they span engines, and they can derive new facts by rule. That is the whole book in miniature: an ETL-free business view over many databases, where the ontology supplies the meaning and the mappings supply the connections. "ETL-free" means no copying, not no work: the effort moves into agreeing on the vocabulary and on how each system's keys line up.

P.3The stack at a glance

Every technology in the book, in one table. Don't memorize it; come back when a name stops making sense.

NameWhat it isWhy it existsCh.
RDFA data model where every fact is a three-part sentence: subject, predicate, object (a "triple").Every database invents its own shape: tables and columns here, JSON there. Combining two of them normally means first reconciling their shapes, which is what ETL does. If every fact is written as one simple sentence (customer 225 lives in Milwaukee), facts from Aurora and Redshift already have the same shape and can simply sit side by side.2–3
SPARQLThe query language for RDF: patterns of triples with placeholders (like ?city) that Stardog fills in from the data.SQL asks "which rows in these tables match?" Triples have no tables, only sentences linked by shared names. So you need a language that asks "find things connected like this: customer → address → city". SPARQL is that language, and it's a W3C standard, like SQL is an ANSI one.4
RDFSA small vocabulary for describing classes and labels.SQL splits the world in two: DDL (CREATE TABLE) defines the shape, DML (INSERT, SELECT) handles records, and you read the shape through DESCRIBE. In RDF there is no split. The shape is written as triples too ("Customer is a class"), so the same INSERT DATA creates the schema or a record, and the same SELECT reads either one, or both in one query. The catch: declaring a shape doesn't enforce anything (that's SHACL's job, Chapter 11).5
OWLA richer vocabulary on top of RDFS: property types, inverses, and more.RDFS can say that a Customer exists, but not much about how things relate. OWL adds the detail: "purchasedBy links an Order to a Customer", "placedOrder is the same link read backwards (Customer → Order)". Stardog can then answer questions that the data doesn't state directly.6
SMS2 / R2RMLMapping languages that say "each row of this SQL table becomes these triples" (SMS2 is Stardog's; R2RML, short for RDB to RDF Mapping Language, is the W3C standard).Your data sits in SQL tables, but the ontology speaks in sentences. A mapping is the translator: "each row of c360.customer is a Customer, and its first_name column is the Customer's firstName." Without one, Stardog doesn't know your tables exist.8
Virtual graphA named graph whose triples are produced on demand from SQL through a mapping.Copying data into a new store (ETL) means duplicates, pipelines to maintain and answers that go stale. A virtual graph leaves the rows in Aurora and Redshift and turns your question into SQL at the moment you ask.7–9
Stardog rulesIF-THEN statements that derive new facts at query time.Otherwise every report, dashboard and AI prompt re-implements "big spender" slightly differently, and the numbers disagree. Write the definition once, in the model, and every query uses the same one.10
SHACLA W3C language for validation rules ("every Customer has exactly one email").RDFS and OWL describe data but never reject it: a customer with no email is simply accepted. SHACL is the checker. Like NOT NULL and CHECK constraints, it reports every record that breaks your rules.11
VoiceboxStardog's built-in assistant that turns questions into SPARQL using your ontology.So people who don't know SPARQL, such as business users or an AI agent, can ask "who are our top spenders?" in plain English and still get an answer grounded in the ontology.13
MCPModel Context Protocol, an open standard connecting AI applications to tools and data.Every AI app used to need custom glue code for every tool it called. MCP is a standard plug, so any agent can call Stardog (or any other service) the same way, like JDBC for AI tools.13–14

P.4Why bother with a semantic layer?

You could answer the Wisconsin question without any of this: export customers from Aurora, load them into Redshift, write the JOIN. For one question, that's the right call. The semantic layer earns its keep when there are many questions, systems and consumers, especially when one consumer is an AI model that has to write the query itself:

  • Meaning. The vocabulary is written down once, precisely, in a form people and programs can read. An LLM told "an Order is purchased by a Customer" writes far better queries than one given forty cryptic table names. (ch5–6)
  • Federation. Data stays where it lives: no pipeline to build or pay for, no stale copy. (ch7–9)
  • One definition. "Big spender" is defined once, as a rule, and every query, dashboard and agent gets the same answer. (ch10)
  • Governance. "This role can't see SSNs" is stated once, for all sources, instead of as grants in every copy of the data. (ch12)

There are costs: federated queries are slower than local ones, an ontology is one more artifact to maintain, and heavy aggregation over billions of rows still belongs in the warehouse. Chapter 7 lays out the trade-offs.

This book follows the AWS Machine Learning blog post "Build a semantic layer for agentic AI on AWS with Stardog and Amazon Bedrock AgentCore" (July 2026). This book is the long version, for someone who has never touched RDF. One deliberate difference: the blog's agent runs on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore; yours uses an OpenAI or OpenRouter model. Chapter 14 shows how the two map onto each other.

P.5How we'll build it: the build track

"Building an ontology solution" is five layers of work, each on the one before: learn the data model (triples, names, SPARQL); write the vocabulary (the ontology); connect it to the real tables (mappings and virtual graphs); add meaning and control (rules, validation, security); and put the agent on top. Eight chapters leave something permanent in Stardog; we call them build steps. Everything else is explanation, or practice in a scratch area deleted at the end of the chapter.

P.6What's already done

The plumbing is in place and tested, so the book can spend its time on ideas instead of network settings:

  • AWS. Aurora PostgreSQL 16 Serverless v2 (database c360, schema c360) with 500 customers, their addresses, credit cards and rewards accounts; Redshift Serverless (database dev, schema c360) with 2,500 purchases, 580 products, 31 categories and 418 vendors. Both cost next to nothing when idle (Aurora pauses after five idle minutes).
  • Network. The security group admits only Stardog Cloud's outbound address and your laptop.
  • DBeaver. Connections to both databases, to check any answer the old way.
  • Stardog. A Stardog Cloud Free-tier endpoint with a database kit-c360 and two data sources (saved JDBC connections), aurora_c360 and redshift_c360.

And, deliberately, nothing else. kit-c360 is empty: zero triples, no virtual graphs, no ontology. An earlier session built the whole thing end to end (the $502,529.42 above comes from that run, and matches SQL to the cent), then took it back down to bare connectivity so you can build every piece yourself.

The first query of the day is slow. Aurora takes roughly 15–20 seconds to wake from its pause: the price of paying $0 while idle, not a bug. Redshift also spins up on demand, with a 60-second billing minimum.

P.8How to read this book

Slowly, with your hands on the keyboard. Keep Studio open beside the page. When you reach a green "Try it in Studio" box, stop and do it: paste the query, run it, compare with what the box says to expect. The steps aren't done for you, on purpose. By Chapter 14 you'll have built every part of the system yourself, and you'll be able to rebuild or change it without this book.

The boxes each have one job. If you know SQL gives the nearest SQL idea and where the analogy breaks; If you know Java does the same for readers who think in objects. Mental model gives you a picture to keep. Try it in Studio is hands-on: practice steps use scratch graphs such as <urn:book:ch02> and clean up; build steps leave something permanent. Gotcha is a real pitfall in this exact setup, one you would hit if nobody warned you. In the AWS blog ties the chapter to the blog.

Every chapter ends with check-yourself questions and a recap. Ask Claude whenever something is unclear; there's no ceremony. The one exception is the build-step chapters: they end with a "Build step N done" button. Paste that to Claude with what you saw (the counts or results), so we can confirm your build matches before the next step relies on it. Studio's screens change between versions, so mention anything that looked different.

You'll see "like a JOIN" and "like a view" a lot. They're footholds, not claims of sameness. The biggest adjustment for a database engineer (Chapter 5) is how a missing fact is read. Say the data has no phone number for Carolyn. A database reads that as Carolyn has no phone. This world reads it as we don't know Carolyn's phone: it just isn't written down here, and another source might have it.

This book owes its shape to that post by the AWS and Stardog teams, which fit the whole system into one afternoon's reading; this is my attempt to fit it into one engineer's head. Let's build it.

Check yourself

Where does Carolyn's total of $502,529.42 actually get computed?

At query time, in Stardog's engine: it rewrites the question as SQL for Redshift (purchases) and Aurora (name and state), runs both, and joins and sums the results. Nothing is copied ahead of time.

What does Stardog store about your customers?

No customer rows at all. It stores meaning: the ontology, rules, security settings, and mappings to the tables in Aurora and Redshift.

Which part of the system knows that orders live in Redshift?

The mappings. The ontology says what an Order is; the redshift_c360 mapping says rows of c360.purchase become Orders.

Which chapters leave something permanent in Stardog?

The build steps: ch5, ch6, ch8, ch9, ch10 and ch12, plus ch14, whose agent lives on your laptop, and ch15, which adds a third source, a registry and a checked agent. The rest is explanation or scratch practice.

Why is there a separate stardog_admin user?

The portal login (your email) doesn't work against the endpoint; Studio, the API and the agent need a native user with the cloud role.

Preface recap

  • The goal: a plain-English question goes to an OpenAI/OpenRouter agent, which asks Stardog in SPARQL, which answers live from Aurora and Redshift with no ETL.
  • Stardog holds meaning (ontology, rules, security, mappings), not copies of the data.
  • The mappings know where data lives; Stardog rewrites SPARQL into SQL per source and joins the results itself.
  • Eight chapters are build steps; AWS, DBeaver and the two data sources already work, and kit-c360 is deliberately empty.
  • Read beside Studio, do every "Try it" yourself, ask questions any time, and report your results after each build step.
